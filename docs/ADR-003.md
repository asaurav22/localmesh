# ADR-003: Pull-Based vs Push-Based Health Checks

## Context
The Control Plane needs to know whether registered services are actually alive and responding — not just whether they recently registered. A service could register successfully and then crash a second later. Without active health monitoring, dead services would stay in the registry until their TTL expires, causing unnecessary routing failures during that window.

A mechanism is needed to detect and evict dead services faster than TTL expiry allows.

## Decision
Use pull-based health checks. The Control Plane actively polls each registered service's GET /health endpoint every 10 seconds. A service that misses 3 consecutive checks is evicted from the registry immediately — regardless of its remaining TTL.

## Alternatives Considered

**Push-based heartbeats from services**
Each service sends a periodic heartbeat to the Control Plane independently. This is the approach used by Netflix Eureka and is conceptually simpler — if heartbeats stop, the service is considered dead.

The problem: push-based heartbeats require every service to implement a heartbeat client. This creates coupling between the service and the mesh infrastructure. Adding a new service means remembering to add heartbeat logic. Forgetting means silent failures in health tracking.

**TTL-only expiry (no active checks)**
Rely entirely on TTL expiry and periodic re-registration via heartbeats. Simple but slow — a crashed service stays in the registry for up to 30 seconds before its TTL expires. During that window the sidecar will attempt to route traffic to a dead service, triggering circuit breaker trips and unnecessary errors.

## Reasoning
Pull-based checks require only a GET /health endpoint on each service — a convention so universal it is essentially free. No additional client code is needed in the services themselves. The Control Plane bears the full responsibility of health tracking, which is architecturally correct — health monitoring is an infrastructure concern, not a service concern.

LocalMesh already implements service-side heartbeats for TTL renewal (every 20 seconds). Adding push-based health checks on top would create two separate mechanisms that could drift out of sync. A single pull-based monitor is simpler and sufficient.

## Trade-offs Accepted
- Network overhead scales linearly O(n) with the number of registered services. At 1000 services polling every 10 seconds, the Control Plane sends 100 requests per second just for health  checks. This is impractical at scale.
- Health check latency: a crashed service takes up to 30 seconds to be evicted (3 checks × 10s interval). Push-based heartbeats with a short interval could detect failures faster.
- The Control Plane becomes a single point of failure for health monitoring. If it goes down, no health checks run.

## Production Equivalent
**Consul** uses a hybrid approach — services register with a health check definition and Consul's agent runs the checks locally on each node, distributing the polling load. **Kubernetes** uses liveness probes defined in the Pod spec, executed by the kubelet on each node — again distributed. At scale, centralised polling as implemented here would be replaced by a distributed health checking system to avoid the O(n) bottleneck at the Control Plane.
